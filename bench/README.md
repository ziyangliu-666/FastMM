## How to read the table

- **per iteration, fastest run.** Google Benchmark divides the time of a repetition by its iteration
  count, so every figure here is a *mean* over the iterations of one repetition; the column shows
  the smallest such mean over every repetition of every round. Interference only makes a benchmark
  slower, so on a machine shared with other work the fastest repetition is the least disturbed
  estimate. It cannot show how much one iteration differs from the next. What one iteration is
  differs per benchmark: for the codec benchmarks it is a batch of 64 operations, for
  `BM_L2_ApplyDelta/20` a 20-level delta, for `BM_EngineStep_Sim` a 32-event step. Divide before
  comparing to a per-operation figure.
- **CPU there.** The process CPU time of that same repetition. It should nearly equal the wall
  figure beside it for anything that does not wait; where it does not, even the fastest repetition
  was descheduled and the row should not be quoted. Two exceptions by construction: the
  `/real_time` rows wait on a socket or another thread, and the `/manual_time` rows report only the
  `rdtsc` interval as wall time while their CPU time also counts the untimed setup between
  iterations.
- **median over runs (min – max).** The median of the repetition means and their full range. This is
  the run-to-run spread on this machine, and it is the only dispersion Google Benchmark's own output
  supports. The published table used to carry a "stddev" column: it was the standard deviation of
  five repetition means, which is why 60 rows read `0.0 ns`. It is gone.
- **per-op p50 / p99.** Only for the benchmarks that time every operation themselves with `rdtsc`
  and keep a `LogLinearHistogram`: the reactor, UDP and tick-to-order benchmarks (counters `p50` /
  `p99`) and the codec benchmarks (counters `p50_ns` / `p99_ns`, per operation inside the batch).
  These are real per-operation percentiles. Every other row has none, and the cell is empty rather
  than a zero. The histogram's buckets are about 2 % wide, so these values are quantised.
- **throughput.** `items_per_second` or `bytes_per_second` as the benchmark set them.

Two benchmarks run a second thread inside the pinned pass instead of declaring they need two cores:
`BM_SpscRing_Throughput` and `BM_SpscRing_PingPong` pin their partner thread themselves, to a core
chosen from the machine's core count rather than from the affinity mask, so the partner leaves the
pinned core deliberately. Their figures are cross-core figures. If a future cgroup cpuset makes that
call fail the partner would land on the benchmark's own core and the ping-pong would read in
milliseconds; there is no check for it.

What the numbers do not include: no benchmark here crosses a network, a TLS session or a venue.
`BM_TickToOrder_Sim` is the engine inside the simulator; the section below says exactly what is in
and out of its timed region.

Benchmarks whose loop carries no dependency from one iteration to the next measure throughput, not
latency: the machine overlaps several of them. Where the quantity of interest is the latency of one
operation, the benchmark now chains the result into the next iteration and says so in its source
comment. It cost `BM_Fixed_RoundToTick` 0.7 -> 3.6 ns and `BM_Fixed_Mul` 0.8 -> 2.2 ns; the
other two, `BM_L2_UpdateNearTop` (2.2 ns) and `BM_Risk_CheckNew_Pass` (6.5 ns), did not move,
because the book's `ClobberMemory()` and the risk checks' own data flow already serialised them.
The chain costs an `and` and an `add` on the critical path, so about half a nanosecond of those
figures is the measurement. `BM_Risk_CheckNew_Killed`, `BM_Fixed_FromDecimal`, `BM_Fixed_ToDecimal`
and `BM_L2_InsertEraseTop` stay throughput measurements, and their figures are lower than the
latency of one call.

`benchmark::DoNotOptimize` on a value inside such a loop adds a store to the chain (it costs
`BM_Fixed_RoundToTick` another 3.5 ns), so these four call it once after the loop on the chained
variable instead. Watch for the opposite failure: with the chain taken from the low bit of a result
that is always a multiple of the tick, gcc proved the bit constant and folded the whole
`BM_Fixed_RoundToTick` loop away, and it reported 0.000 ns.

Measurement conditions, and what could not be controlled: WSL2 under Hyper-V, no `isolcpus`, no
cpufreq control (no governor or boost interface is exposed, so the clock is whatever the host gives
us), no huge-page or ASLR control (Google Benchmark warns about ASLR on every run), and other work
on the machine during the runs. The spread column is the honest statement of what that did to each
row. p99 columns include host scheduling stalls and should be read as "this machine's tail", not the
code's.

The rows do not all come from the same fifteen minutes: `bench_book`, `bench_fixed`, `bench_risk`
and `bench_tick_to_order` were re-run (`scripts/bench.sh --only`) after their sources changed, on a
quieter machine, which is why their spread is a few percent and the rest of the table's is not.

## The rows worth quoting, and what they contain

**Tick to order, `BM_TickToOrder_Sim`: 111 to 123 ns.** That is the p50 of the per-tick `rdtsc`
histogram; it read 111, 123 and 123 ns in three rounds this morning and 111 ns in all three of a
later set, with p99 151 to 167 ns. The fastest repetition mean is 116.3 ns. In it: a `BookDeltaMsg`
pushed into the feed, `Engine::step()` (L2 apply, BasicMM, quote manager, risk, OMS, message
serialisation) and `SimTransport::send` for each of the two cancels the
tick produces, which copies the message into a 192-byte scheduler slot and pushes it on the arrival
heap. Not in it: the network, TLS, JSON, the venue, acks, and the untimed settle loop between ticks.
`BM_TickToOrder_SimHash` is the same tick with the simulator's outbound SHA-256 on: 287 to 303 ns
p50, so the checksum is 170 to 180 ns of it, for the two messages a tick sends. That hash is a
determinism check of the simulator; the engine does not compute it, in the simulator or live.

This file used to publish 839.5 ns "median" and 255 ns p50 for that benchmark. The 839.5 ns was
Google Benchmark's per-iteration time with `PauseTiming`/`ResumeTiming` around the untimed settle
loop -- roughly 600 ns of pause and resume -- and both figures included the checksum. No engine code
changed between those numbers and these.

The per-iteration wall column for the two manual-time benchmarks is the `rdtsc` interval; the CPU
column beside it is not, because process CPU time also counts the untimed settle loop. Read the
p50 column for these two rows.

**Reactor round trips.** With the client and echo server on one reactor
(`BM_ReactorEchoInline`), 12.3 to 12.8 µs p50, the same for epoll and io_uring. With the echo server
on its own thread (`BM_ReactorEchoThread`, run unpinned), busy polling gives 10.75 µs p50 on both
backends -- but the median over runs is 251 µs and the worst repetition 23.9 ms: two busy-polling
threads need two free cores, and this host does not reliably have them. Blocking waits give 19.5 to
20.5 µs p50 and a 74 to 77 µs median over runs, since each round trip costs two cross-thread
wake-ups. The 8.00 ms rows this file published for the busy variants on 2026-09-23 were that failure
mode made permanent: the benchmark was pinned to one core together with its echo thread.

**ITCH bridge, working set against capacity.** Per ITCH message, p50: 57.3 ns with a 2^16-order book
and 2,000 to 6,000 resting orders; 61.4 ns with the venue's default 2^20-order book and the same
small stream; 81.9 ns with the default book and 200,000 to 260,000 resting orders. So the capacity
alone costs about 4 ns and the working set about 20 ns. The large stream also produces fewer deltas
per message (0.089 against 0.124) and therefore less downstream work, so the memory effect is
slightly larger than the difference shows. The row this file published as `_DefaultBook` was the
middle case and was described as showing cache and dTLB misses; it did not.

**Rows this host cannot give you.** Anything whose "median over runs" is several times its fastest
run was measured against other work on the same machine: on this run that includes `BM_Matching_*`,
`BM_Fix_*`, `BM_Oms_*`, `BM_QuoteManager_*`, `BM_Histogram_*`, `BM_Log_*` and the `BM_Json_*`
private-message rows. Their fastest-run column is still the best estimate available, but the spread
is the machine, not the code, and no p99 in this file is the code's tail. `BM_Ouch50_EncodeColdMap`
is the extreme case (fastest 2.10 µs, worst repetition 632 µs): it faults in a fresh `UserRefMap`
per batch, so it measures the allocator and the kernel as much as the encoder.

`bench_order_tcp` isolates the send call over a veth (one thread, the server in a second namespace, 64-byte messages): `write` on a `TCP_NODELAY` socket 3.97 µs p50, 7.9 µs until the server's `read` returns.
